GENERAL HEALTH INFORMATION FOR PATIENTS
========================================

UNDERSTANDING YOUR VITAL SIGNS
Vital signs are basic measurements that give the medical team a snapshot of your body's condition.

Blood Pressure:
Normal: 90/60 mmHg to 120/80 mmHg
High (Hypertension): 140/90 mmHg or above
Low (Hypotension): Below 90/60 mmHg
High blood pressure over time increases the risk of heart attack, stroke, and kidney disease. Low blood pressure can cause dizziness and fainting.

Heart Rate (Pulse):
Normal adult resting rate: 60 to 100 beats per minute
Below 60 bpm: Bradycardia (slow heart rate) — can be normal in athletes but may need investigation
Above 100 bpm: Tachycardia (fast heart rate) — may indicate infection, dehydration, anxiety, or heart problems

Temperature:
Normal: 36.1°C to 37.2°C (97°F to 99°F)
Low-grade fever: 37.3°C to 38°C
Fever: Above 38°C (100.4°F)
High fever: Above 39.5°C (103°F) — needs prompt medical attention

Respiratory Rate (Breathing):
Normal adult: 12 to 20 breaths per minute
Above 20 breaths per minute at rest may indicate infection, heart problems, or respiratory issues.

Oxygen Saturation (SpO2):
Normal: 95% to 100%
Below 95%: May indicate breathing difficulty — seek medical attention
Below 90%: Medical emergency — seek help immediately

COMMON CONDITIONS EXPLAINED

HYPERTENSION (HIGH BLOOD PRESSURE)
What it is: When the force of blood against artery walls is consistently too high.
Why it matters: Often has no symptoms but significantly increases risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, and vision problems.
Management: Lifestyle changes (reducing salt intake, regular exercise, weight management, limiting alcohol), and medications prescribed by a doctor.
Important: Never stop blood pressure medication without consulting your doctor, even if you feel fine.

DIABETES
What it is: A condition where the body cannot properly regulate blood sugar levels.
Type 1: The body produces little or no insulin. Usually diagnosed in childhood or young adulthood.
Type 2: The body does not use insulin effectively. Most common type, often linked to lifestyle factors.
Symptoms: Increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, tingling in hands or feet.
Management: Healthy diet (low sugar and refined carbohydrates), regular physical activity, monitoring blood sugar, and medications or insulin as prescribed.
Emergency signs: Very high blood sugar (hyperglycemia) — extreme thirst, nausea, fruity breath smell. Very low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) — shakiness, sweating, confusion, faintness — give sugar immediately and seek help.

ASTHMA
What it is: A chronic condition causing inflammation and narrowing of the airways, making breathing difficult.
Symptoms: Wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, coughing (especially at night or early morning).
Triggers: Dust, pollen, cold air, smoke, exercise, respiratory infections, stress.
Management: Avoid known triggers, use reliever inhaler (usually blue) during an attack, use preventer inhaler (usually brown or orange) daily as prescribed.
Emergency: If reliever inhaler does not work after 10 puffs or breathing remains very difficult — call emergency services.

MALARIA (COMMON IN TROPICAL REGIONS)
What it is: A serious infectious disease caused by parasites transmitted through mosquito bites.
Symptoms: Fever, chills, sweating, headache, nausea, vomiting, muscle pain. Symptoms typically appear 10 to 15 days after infection.
Seek emergency care if: High fever with confusion, seizures, difficulty breathing, or yellowing of the skin (jaundice).
Prevention: Use mosquito nets, repellents, and protective clothing. Take antimalarial medication if recommended for your region.

TYPHOID FEVER
What it is: A bacterial infection caused by Salmonella typhi, spread through contaminated food and water.
Symptoms: Sustained high fever, weakness, stomach pain, headache, diarrhea or constipation, rose-colored spots on the torso in some cases.
Management: Requires antibiotic treatment prescribed by a doctor. Rest and adequate hydration are important.
Prevention: Drink clean water, eat properly cooked food, practice good hand hygiene.

URINARY TRACT INFECTION (UTI)
What it is: A bacterial infection of the bladder, urethra, or kidneys.
Symptoms: Burning or pain when urinating, frequent urge to urinate even when little comes out, cloudy or foul-smelling urine, pelvic pain (in women), fever (if the kidneys are affected).
Management: Antibiotic treatment prescribed by a doctor. Drink plenty of water.
Important: If left untreated, a UTI can spread to the kidneys and become serious.

ANEMIA
What it is: A condition where you do not have enough healthy red blood cells to carry adequate oxygen around the body.
Symptoms: Fatigue, weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness, cold hands and feet, irregular heartbeat.
Common causes: Iron deficiency (most common), vitamin B12 deficiency, blood loss, chronic disease.
Management: Depends on the cause. Iron-rich foods, supplements, or treating underlying conditions.

HEALTHY HABITS THAT SUPPORT YOUR RECOVERY AND WELLBEING

HYDRATION
Adults should drink about 1.5 to 2 litres of water per day minimum. More during illness, fever, diarrhea, or physical activity. Signs of dehydration: dark yellow urine, dry mouth, headache, dizziness. Avoid sugary drinks and excessive caffeine during illness.

SLEEP
Adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Poor sleep weakens the immune system and slows recovery. Rest is an important part of healing.

NUTRITION DURING ILLNESS
Eat light, easily digestible foods (boiled rice, toast, bananas, soups) if you have an upset stomach. Avoid greasy, fried, or spicy foods during illness. Continue eating even when unwell — your body needs energy to fight infection.

WHEN TO SEEK MEDICAL HELP WITHOUT DELAY
Go to the hospital or see a doctor promptly if:
- Your symptoms are getting significantly worse
- You have a high fever that does not come down with medication
- You are unable to keep fluids down for more than 12 hours
- You are in severe pain
- You notice blood where it should not be (urine, stool, vomit)
- A child under 3 months has any fever
- An elderly person is confused, unusually sleepy, or has fallen

MENTAL HEALTH MATTERS
Mental health is part of overall health. It is normal to feel anxious, sad, or overwhelmed sometimes — especially during illness.
Seek help if: You feel persistently sad or hopeless, you have thoughts of harming yourself, you are unable to function in daily life, or you are experiencing severe anxiety or panic attacks.
Talk to a doctor — mental health conditions are treatable and there is no shame in seeking help.

RIGHTS OF PATIENTS
Every patient has the right to:
- Be treated with dignity and respect regardless of age, gender, ethnicity, or background
- Receive clear information about their condition and treatment options
- Give or withhold consent before any procedure
- Ask questions and receive understandable answers
- Confidentiality of their medical information
- Receive care without discrimination
- A second medical opinion if they choose

If you feel your rights are not being respected, speak to the hospital's patient services or complaints department.
