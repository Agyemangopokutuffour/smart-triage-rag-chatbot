PATIENT FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS (FAQs)
==========================================

ARRIVAL AND REGISTRATION

Q: What do I do when I first arrive at the hospital?
A: Go directly to the reception or front desk. You will be asked to register your name, contact details, and reason for visit. After registration, you will be directed to the triage nurse who will assess how urgently you need care. Do not sit down and wait without registering first — unregistered patients do not get seen.

Q: What documents should I bring to the hospital?
A: Bring a valid photo ID (national ID, passport, or driver's licence), your health insurance card if you have one, any previous medical records or prescriptions that are relevant to your current condition, and a list of any medications you are currently taking. If you are coming for a follow-up visit, bring your previous discharge summary or referral letter.

Q: Can someone come with me to the hospital?
A: Yes. It is recommended to bring a trusted person with you, especially if you are unwell, elderly, or may need support during your visit. Only one or two companions may be allowed in clinical areas depending on the hospital's policy.

Q: I do not speak the local language well. Can I still get help?
A: Yes. Inform the front desk staff and they will do their best to assist you. Many hospitals have staff who speak multiple languages. If needed, a family member or trusted person may assist with translation.

WAITING TIMES

Q: Why is the waiting time so long even though I am unwell?
A: Patients are seen based on medical urgency, not arrival time. Someone who arrived after you but is in a more critical condition will be seen first. This system, called triage, exists to save lives. If your condition worsens while you wait, tell the triage nurse or front desk immediately.

Q: How long will I wait to see a doctor?
A: Wait times depend on how busy the hospital is and your triage level. Emergency cases (Level 1) are seen immediately. Urgent cases (Level 2) wait about 15 to 30 minutes. Less urgent cases (Level 3) wait 30 to 60 minutes. Non-urgent cases (Level 4) may wait 1 to 2 hours or more. These are estimates and may vary.

Q: What should I do if I feel my condition is getting worse while waiting?
A: Do not wait quietly. Go directly to the triage nurse or front desk and tell them your symptoms have changed or worsened. They will re-assess you and may move you to a higher priority level.

Q: Can I leave and come back if the wait is too long?
A: You can leave, but it is strongly advised against if you came in for a medical concern. If you leave before being seen, you do so at your own risk. You will need to re-register when you return and your place in the queue will be reset.

SEEING THE DOCTOR

Q: How long will my appointment with the doctor take?
A: A typical consultation lasts between 10 and 30 minutes depending on the complexity of your condition. If you need tests or imaging, you may need to wait longer for results before the doctor can complete your assessment.

Q: Can I ask for a specific doctor?
A: In most cases, especially in emergency or outpatient settings, you will be seen by whichever doctor is available. You can request a specific doctor for scheduled appointments, but this is not guaranteed.

Q: What should I tell the doctor?
A: Be honest and as detailed as possible. Tell the doctor: what symptoms you have and when they started, how severe the symptoms are on a scale of 1 to 10, whether the symptoms are constant or come and go, what makes them better or worse, any medications you are taking (prescription and over-the-counter), any allergies you have, any recent illnesses, surgeries, or hospital visits, and your family medical history if relevant.

Q: What if I do not understand what the doctor said?
A: It is your right to ask for clarification. Tell the doctor you did not understand and ask them to explain again in simpler terms. You can also ask a nurse to help explain your diagnosis or treatment plan after your consultation.

MEDICATIONS AND PRESCRIPTIONS

Q: Where do I collect my medication?
A: Take your prescription slip to the hospital pharmacy. Queue at the pharmacy window, hand in your slip, and wait for your name to be called. The pharmacist will dispense your medication and explain how to take it. Always ask the pharmacist if you have questions about your medicines.

Q: What if I cannot afford my medication?
A: Speak to the social welfare or patient services department. Many hospitals have programmes to assist patients who cannot afford treatment or medication. Do not skip your medication without speaking to someone first.

Q: Can I take my own medication while in hospital?
A: Only if approved by the doctor or nurse. Inform the medical team of all medications you are currently taking, including herbal remedies and supplements. Taking unapproved medication while in hospital can interfere with your treatment.

TESTS AND RESULTS

Q: The doctor sent me for blood tests. How long will results take?
A: Basic blood tests usually take 2 to 6 hours. More specialized tests can take 24 to 48 hours or longer. The doctor or nurse will let you know when to expect results. Do not leave the hospital without asking when and how you will receive your results.

Q: What happens if a test shows something serious?
A: The doctor will speak with you privately to explain the findings and discuss what happens next. If you are concerned or scared, ask the doctor or nurse to explain clearly and take your time to understand. You have the right to ask questions.

ADMISSION AND DISCHARGE

Q: What does it mean if I am admitted?
A: Being admitted means you need to stay in the hospital for further treatment, observation, or care. You will be assigned a bed in a ward appropriate to your condition. A nurse will guide you through the process and explain what to expect.

Q: How do I know when I am ready to be discharged?
A: The doctor will decide when it is safe for you to go home. Before discharge, you should receive a clear explanation of your diagnosis, instructions for home care, a list of medications to take, follow-up appointment details, and warning signs to watch out for. Do not leave without this information.

Q: What is a discharge summary?
A: A discharge summary is a document you receive when leaving the hospital. It contains your diagnosis, the treatment you received, your medication list, and instructions for follow-up. Keep this document — you will need it for follow-up appointments or if you visit another doctor.

EMERGENCIES

Q: What are the signs of a medical emergency?
A: Seek emergency help immediately if you or someone else experiences: chest pain or tightness, difficulty breathing, unconsciousness or unresponsiveness, signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech), severe bleeding that does not stop, suspected overdose, severe allergic reaction (throat swelling), or a seizure that does not stop.

Q: What should I do if someone collapses near me in the hospital?
A: Shout for help immediately. Call out to any hospital staff nearby. Do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger. If no staff are visible, go to the nearest nursing station or press an emergency call button.
